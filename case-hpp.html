Description

In-house design work for Hospital Pequeno Príncipe — Brazil’s largest exclusively pediatric hospital. The projects are the hospital’s; the design decisions are mine.

Context

Design and front-end delivery, one to two weeks per page, inside a platform that didn’t want to do half of what the briefs asked for.

The hospital’s landing pages drew ~98,000 visitors and ~17,700 leads in H1 2026.

02Why this hospital matters

Hospital Pequeno Príncipe is the largest exclusively pediatric hospital in Brazil. Around 60% of its care goes to public-health (SUS) patients. It’s recognised by the Ministry of Health as a reference centre for rare diseases — roughly 30% of its procedures — and receives children from every region of the country.

A hospital that treats mostly public patients runs on donations. Every page here sits somewhere in that funnel, which means layout decisions are funding decisions.

04Verão Maior

The context is literal. This page is read at the beach — one hand, sunlight on the screen, a child in the frame. Anything that needed sustained attention was going to lose.

The decision. The four safety topics became illustrated, self-contained blocks instead of running text, each readable in isolation and out of order. A parent who reads only the sunscreen block still got what the page was for. Then the donation ask sits below the useful content, not above it.

The trade-off. Burying the ask costs immediate conversion. This page existed to deliver campaign information for a state partnership — asking early would have spent the goodwill the campaign was there to build.

The constraint. Visual identity inherited from Verão Maior Paraná, a state government campaign carried across physical products. Third-party brand, non-negotiable.

05Pix Automático

Eleven questions between someone and a recurring donation.

The problem. Authorising an automatic monthly debit means handing over ongoing access to your bank account. Communications answered eleven objections. My job was making sure a reader could find their objection fast enough to keep going.

The decision. Eleven collapsed questions read as a wall and get abandoned; eleven expanded questions get skimmed and lose the person before the end. I built it as scannable question headers, sized to be the only thing you need to read to navigate, with the answers subordinate — so someone scrolling for “can I cancel?” hits it in seconds. The activation walkthrough was pulled out of the FAQ rhythm entirely and given the visual weight of a final step, because it’s the only item on the page that ends somewhere else: inside the reader’s banking app.

The trade-off. A long page contradicts every rule about landing page length. But this is the page furthest down the funnel — the reader already decided to give. Length that removes doubt is cheaper than brevity that leaves it.

06Mapas

Narrative before ask.

The audience. People who don’t know the hospital exists. Institutional credentials mean nothing to someone with no reason to care yet.

The decision. William’s story — a boy who came from Recife with his mother for a rare disease diagnosis, two bone marrow transplants, a friendship formed during treatment — carries the entire top of the page. The institutional proof (30% rare-disease procedures, 60% SUS, Ministry of Health reference status) sits after it, as confirmation rather than introduction. The mother’s quote gets pull-quote treatment; the statistics get a quiet band. That ordering came from the brief — the weighting is what I controlled.

The trade-off. Someone who arrives already convinced has a long scroll before they can act. Acceptable at the top of the funnel, where the failure mode isn’t friction, it’s indifference.

073% campaign — Excelência

Designing inside someone else’s system.

The stakes. This is the hospital’s largest fundraising campaign — income tax allocation, where a single donor’s decision moves real money. The audience is older, mostly desktop, and evaluating whether an institution deserves a portion of what they’d otherwise pay in tax.

The constraint. The Excelência campaign identity already existed across the institution. My work was applying it to the site’s tax-allocation section within an established structure — the space was defined before I opened the file.

The decision. With expression off the table, the work was legibility and hierarchy: type sized for readers who won’t zoom, a clear path from what the campaign is to how allocation works, and restraint everywhere. Recognisability was worth more than novelty to an audience that has to trust the page before it trusts the institution.

08Building inside the platform

Every page was built in RD Station, which sets the boundaries of what a layout can do. Its editor has a fixed idea of what a landing page looks like, and the briefs regularly asked for something outside it — illustrated content blocks, custom spacing, layouts that hold up on a phone in direct sunlight.

So the pages are part editor, part hand-written HTML: building custom blocks where the editor couldn’t reach, and reworking its output where the default broke the design. The constraint set the floor, not the ceiling.

What this cost, and what it bought. Custom HTML inside a marketing platform is harder to hand over — the next person editing it has to understand what I wrote. In exchange, the pages look designed rather than assembled, which for a hospital asking strangers for money is not a cosmetic difference.

09What I knew and what I didn’t

The scale. The hospital’s landing pages drew ~98,781 visitors and ~17,710 leads in the first half of 2026, at a weighted conversion rate of 17.93%.

The qualification. Those are platform-wide figures for the hospital’s landing pages — not attributed to the four pages here. I didn’t have analytics access. I’m reporting the scale I was designing for, not results I can claim.

What replaced the data. Objectives and audiences declared per brief. Established internal patterns — the summer page repeated a structure proven the year before, the Pix pages followed an existing visual system. Review by communications, marketing, and each department’s director before publishing.

Without analytics, the discipline moves upstream. The decision has to be defensible before it ships, because nothing downstream will tell you whether it was right.

10What the set taught me

  • Constraint is the job.

    Three of the four pages started with the space already defined — a state campaign’s identity, an existing visual system, an institutional campaign look. Copy always arrived approved. The design work lives in what’s left.

  • The ask goes where the trust is.

    Its position changed on every page, and always because of who was arriving.

  • Speed is a design constraint.

    One to two weeks from brief to live means the decision has to be right early. There’s no round of iteration waiting to save it.
