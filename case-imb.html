IMB

A technical catalogue for people who buy machines, not websites.

Description

IMB has been manufacturing concrete extruders and pavers since 1992, from a factory in southern Brazil. Their equipment built the Rodoanel ring road in São Paulo and the Guaratuba bridge in Paraná.

Context

Their website was a Wix page with a duplicated menu, no technical specs, no lead form, and no way to compare models. A buyer researching a machine worth hundreds of thousands had less information available than someone shopping for a kettle.

Who arrives, and what they’re actually doing

This isn’t a consumer audience. High ticket, long cycle, technical and collective decision-making.

  • Civil engineers
  • Site managers
  • Procurement departments
  • Public-sector project managers
  • International distributors

And they’re not browsing. They’re specifying. They arrive knowing the job — 3.6m rigid pavement, New Jersey barrier, curb and gutter — and need to work out which machine does it, at what output, and at what size.

That changes what the site has to do. Persuasion does very little for a technical buyer: what moves the decision is specification density — being able to find, compare and verify.

The best outcome a site can produce here is a buyer who reaches the sales call with the model already chosen.

What the market was doing

IMB named their five main competitors and I went through all five sites. The same pattern held:

  1. 01

    Catalogue as showroom

    The equipment shown, not described.

  2. 02

    Specs buried in a PDF

    The numbers behind a download, if they were published at all.

  3. 03

    No comparison tool

    Model selection pushed entirely onto the salesperson.

The conclusion that shaped the project: in a sector where everyone makes the buyer phone in to learn the basics, serving that information openly and comparably isn’t a convenience — it’s a competitive advantage.

Three decisions

  1. 01

    The comparison tool stopped being a feature and became the product

    The client flagged comparison as the priority. The brief specified two machines side by side, in a table.

    I built it larger: multi-model selection, a fixed comparison bar, attributes aligned row by row with the stronger value visually marked, shareable comparison URLs, and PDF export of the result.

    The PDF wasn’t in the brief and it’s the piece that matters most. Purchasing decisions in construction happen in meetings, from documents, among people who never visited the site. The PDF is how the site walks into a room it wasn’t invited to.

    Trade-offIt’s the most complex thing on an otherwise simple site, and the most sensitive to product-line changes — every new model means maintaining the data that feeds the comparison.

    The comparison toolTwo or more machines side by side, with the comparison bar and the exported PDF.

  2. 02

    Ten pages became five

    The approved sitemap had ten pages: home, catalogue, individual product, comparison, two product-line pages, cases index, individual case, about, contact.

    Approved — ten

    • Home
    • Catalogue
    • Individual product
    • Comparison
    • Product line — extruders
    • Product line — pavers
    • Cases index
    • Individual case
    • About
    • Contact

    Shipped — five

    • Home
    • Catalogue — the two lines became a filter
    • Individual product
    • Comparison
    • Contact — About merged in

    The two line pages became a filter inside the catalogue — they’d have been the same grid with fewer items. “About” was merged into contact, because someone checking whether the supplier is legitimate and someone trying to reach the supplier are the same person, thirty seconds apart.

    Trade-offFewer pages means fewer organic entry points, a real cost for a company exporting to 40+ countries. But eleven content assets were still outstanding when the build started. Fewer pages meant fewer pages waiting on material that might not arrive — and a site that ships beats a site that’s architecturally complete and empty.

  3. 03

    The spec sheet came out of the PDF and became interface

    The industry default is to hand over specifications as a download. I brought the data into the page: structured spec sheets per model, applications as a visual list, and the same attributes organised consistently across every machine — which is what makes comparison possible at all.

    That consistency was the most laborious and least visible part of the project. Standardising attributes across different product lines — an extruder and a paver don’t describe output the same way — is what keeps the comparison tool from becoming a table full of gaps.

    Trade-offIt required a round of technical data validation with the client that consumed project time, and it ties the site to a data format that has to be respected with every new release.

    A spec sheetOne model’s page: the structured specifications and the applications list, in the page rather than behind a download.

The component system

The comparison tool only works if the entire catalogue follows the same data model. So the work started with components, not pages.

  • Equipment card

    Photo, model, applications, one short differentiator, and the comparison control. The same card works in the catalogue, on the home page and in filtered results.

  • Specification row

    The attribute/value pair that repeats across spec sheets and reappears aligned in the comparison table. It’s the component that makes comparison line up.

  • Application badge

    Curb, sidewalk, drainage channel, New Jersey barrier, rigid pavement, custom profile. Works as a label on the card and as a filter in the catalogue.

  • Conversion block

    Quote request and WhatsApp, with consistent hierarchy between primary and secondary action, repeated at every exit point on the site.

Beyond scope, and worth it. Light and dark themes were added because of where the catalogue gets read: on a phone at a construction site in full sun, and in an office at night.

Designing with the content missing

The project started with eleven outstanding content assets, and a two-month deadline.

  • Company history
  • Testimonials
  • Project case studies
  • The country list
  • Professional photography
  • Video
  • Privacy pages required under Brazil’s data protection law

So the structure was designed to absorb absence:

  • Cases as a repeatable component that works with three items or thirty.
  • Social proof that degrades without breaking when there’s one testimonial instead of five.
  • Photography gaps mapped per model — three machines shipped without a secondary gallery because none exists, and the layout doesn’t collapse when that happens.

I also produced a validation checklist for the client: every text, image and data point on the site, listed with an approval status, so review ran as a trackable process instead of scattered emails.

Outcome

The site is live in Portuguese, English and Spanish. The comparison tool, the quote form — integrated with the client’s Ploomes CRM — and the WhatsApp routing were all tested and signed off by IMB’s team before launch.

The launch is recent, so there’s no conversion data yet. What exists so far is client sign-off across every validation track, and the site already operating inside their commercial pipeline. You can see it at imb-brasil.com.br.

What this project taught me

  1. Content constraints are design constraints

    Eleven missing assets aren’t a scheduling problem, they’re a requirement: the structure has to work half-empty.

  2. Data consistency is design work

    The comparison tool isn’t a screen, it’s the consequence of standardising how each machine describes itself. The interface was the easy part.

  3. In technical purchasing, open information is the differentiator

    In a sector where everyone hides the spec sheet behind a PDF, serving it comparably was the most strategic decision in the project — and it doesn’t look like design until you look at the funnel.
